Everyone thinks constraints are the enemy. More money, more time, more options – that's the dream, right? Remove the ceiling and watch yourself go.

That's not how it works.

The best creative work in history came out of brutal limitations. The sonnet has 14 lines. Haiku has 17 syllables. Hemingway bet someone he could write a novel in six words and won ("For sale: baby shoes, never worn."). Twitter's 140-character limit (now 280, and somehow worse) trained an entire generation to cut the fat out of their writing.

None of that would've happened with a blank page and unlimited room.

Freedom is paralyzing. Barry Schwartz wrote a whole book about it – _The Paradox of Choice_. More options, more anxiety. More anxiety, less action. The people with every resource available to them are often the least productive (see: most lottery winners, most trust fund kids, most executives with open-ended mandates).

Constraints force a decision. And a decision is the first step toward anything getting done.

I've seen this play out with money. When I was broke, I got creative. I bartered, I repurposed, I found angles I never would have looked for if I could've just thrown cash at the problem. The constraint made me smarter. The minute I had a little more breathing room, I got lazy. I started solving problems with money instead of thinking.

The constraint was doing work I didn't even realize it was doing.

Same thing with time. The projects I've finished fastest are the ones with hard deadlines – the ones where missing the date actually costs something. Give me six months to do something and I'll spend five of them "preparing." Give me three weeks and I'll ship.

Parkinson's Law: work expands to fill the time available for its completion. Cyril Northcote Parkinson figured this out in 1955. You could've figured it out by looking at your own calendar.

So what do you do with this?

You manufacture constraints. Set a word limit on your writing before you start. Give yourself half the budget you think you need. Ship before you feel ready (you'll never feel ready). Tell people your deadline before you've confirmed you can hit it. Book the venue before you've written the speech.

The goal isn't to make your life harder. The goal is to remove the option of stalling.

Unlimited time is a trap. Unlimited money is a trap. Unlimited options are a trap. The cage that looks like freedom is usually the one that keeps you stuck.

The constraint is the feature. The wall is the thing that forces you to figure out how to climb.
