"Two is one. One is none."

I heard this from Jocko Willink — ex-Navy SEAL, author, and consultant — either in one of his books or on his podcast. The idea is simple: if you only have one of something you depend on, you effectively have none. When it fails, you're done.

There's an older version of this in Ecclesiastes 4:9–10:

> "Two are better than one… If either of them falls, one can help the other up."

That was written thousands of years ago. People are still learning it the hard way.

---

I have two older cars, a 2006 and a 2013. Nothing fancy. I like working on cars, and together they do something one car – no matter how nice – can't do. They give me freedom.

When one goes down for maintenance, I drive the other. When I'm done, I can proactively pull the second one and do the upgrades I've been planning – without pressure, without urgency, without messing up my ability to get around. That's the whole thing.

I know what you're thinking. Why not just get one nicer car? I get it. But that nicer car is still gonna need an oil change. It'll need tires. It'll catch a fender bender at the worst possible time (they always do). And when it does, you're stuck – renting, borrowing, or rearranging your whole week around a car that decided to be unavailable.

Two modest cars beat one great car. The system is better.

---

This applies everywhere, by the way.

Take file backups. You probably have one. Maybe you're using Backblaze, or Dropbox, or Time Machine — something real, not just a copy on the same drive. Good. But what happens when you need to recover a large folder that hasn't been touched in two years and your backup provider says it'll take three days to pull it? That's a real backup. It's also useless to you right now. If you had a second backup — a local drive, a different service with faster restore times — you're up in an hour. If you don't, you wait three days, or you don't recover at all. One backup provider is one. Even when it works.

Touring musicians know this version of the problem. The most expensive guitar in the world is useless if a string snaps mid-set and you can't replace it. Smart touring musicians either carry a second guitar or play something common enough that any music store will have it. The backup is part of the rig, not an afterthought.

Two ways to get somewhere. Two people who know how to run a critical process. Two ways of thinking about a hard problem so you're not stuck when the first one doesn't work. Redundancy isn't pessimism – it's just taking your dependencies seriously.

---

This is what surprised me when I first thought this through.

If you're actually going to do this right – if you're going to have two of the things you depend on – you're going to end up with fewer things overall. You literally can't have two of everything. Nobody has the space, money, or attention for that. So the rule forces you to get honest. What do you actually depend on? What would stop your life if it failed? Those are the things worth doubling up on. Everything else, you probably don't need.

The rule doesn't lead to more stuff. It leads to less stuff, held better.

Two is one. One is none. And nothing is fine – as long as you never needed it.
